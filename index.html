1717–2008 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

The Alford family

Christine Alford Knight grew up in a farming family near Mount Hermon, Louisiana. She became a nurse, moved to Baton Rouge and spent thirty-three years in public health.

Her father’s family had lived near the Bogue Chitto River for generations. This story follows their likely beginnings in colonial North Carolina, their move south, and the lives they built in Louisiana.

PART 1: From North Carolina to the Bogue Chitto

1717–1768 · North Carolina

Julius Alford of North Carolina

Julius Alford lived beside the Tar River in colonial North Carolina. He owned a plantation, was married to Lucy, and named seven children in his will.

He is believed to be the Julius born in New Kent County, Virginia, in September 1717, whose father was James Alford.

We begin with Julius because his records describe his family and farm. His son Jacob is probably the man who later settled near the Bogue Chitto—the likely connection to Christine’s Louisiana family.

1768–1771 · North Carolina

Julius provides for his family

Julius died by 1771. His will provided for Lucy and the children, including money from the estate’s income for their upkeep and schooling.

Jacob was to inherit the home plantation on both sides of the Tar River, along with livestock and a breeding mare. This was the family property he would later sell as he moved away.

About 1785–1789 · North Carolina

Jacob starts a family

Jacob is believed to have married Elizabeth Bryant around 1785, while still in North Carolina. A later published family Bible transcription names Elizabeth as his wife and the mother of Needham, born in 1789.

Jacob was already a father before the move south.

1789 · Franklin County, North Carolina

Leaving the Tar River

Over several years, Jacob sold land he had inherited from Julius. He also sold his right to a separate share of his father’s estate that would come to him after his mother’s death.

That 1789 agreement identifies him in his own words: “the son of Julius Alford deceased.”

1790–1795 · Cumberland County, North Carolina

A move and a second marriage

By 1790, Jacob was living in Cumberland County, farther south in North Carolina, and selling the remaining Tar River property.

Family histories place his second marriage there, around 1792, to Franky, usually called Frances Seaborn. We do not know what happened to Elizabeth or exactly when the second marriage took place.

A Jacob Alford—probably the same man—bought land in Cumberland in 1791 and sold it in 1795.

1797–1806 · Montgomery County, Georgia

The Georgia years

Jacob’s likely route next takes him to Montgomery County, Georgia. Records place a man of his name there from 1797 to 1806, holding land and serving on juries. A tax list also records two enslaved people, without naming them.

He was probably the Jacob who would settle near the Bogue Chitto in 1807. A later family account remembers a move from South Carolina; the precise route is still uncertain.

December 1807 · Bogue Chitto · Spanish West Florida

Settling beside the Bogue Chitto

Jacob settled beside the Bogue Chitto River, just south of today’s Louisiana–Mississippi line. His land claim dates the settlement to December 1807.

This was still Spanish West Florida. The United States had made the Louisiana Purchase four years earlier, but Spain still governed this disputed region.

Here the family put down roots. The later generations we follow would live within a few miles of this tract.

1810–1812 · Bogue Chitto region

The government changes

In 1810, a rebellion against Spanish rule was followed by United States annexation of this part of West Florida. Louisiana became a state in 1812, and the region was incorporated into it that year.

Families already living here now faced a practical question: how would the new government recognize their land claims?

1813–1819 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

From settlement to recognized claim

Jacob’s claim rested on settlement: he had occupied the land before receiving federal recognition of his right to it. A government list compiled in 1813 records his claim.

In 1819, Congress allowed qualifying settlers on these lists to receive land grants—even without written title—if they had lived on or cultivated the land before the required date. The limit was generally one tract of 640 acres.

Jacob’s claim was confirmed under that law. The survey would come later.

1824–1830 · Washington Parish, Louisiana

After Jacob’s death

A published family Bible transcription dates Jacob’s death to 1824. Franky, believed to be his widow, appears as the head of a Washington Parish household in 1830, with younger people whose ages fit several of the children.

The family remained in the area. We have not established who was still living on Jacob’s original tract, or when it passed out of family hands.

1831 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

The survey of Jacob’s land

On 20 January 1831, a survey was made for Jacob Alford’s heirs. It measured 636.54 acres—almost a square mile.

The drawing shows the Bogue Chitto along the eastern boundary and the Louisiana–Mississippi line along the north. Later surveys and maps let us locate this tract today.

This is the land associated with the family’s first settlement beside the river.

John would later acquire separate land about two to three miles southwest, in the same township. His family’s farms and burial places would cluster nearby.

Our story now follows John and Margaret, Christine’s great-grandparents.

PART 2: John, Margaret, Seaborn & Laura

1833 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

John and Margaret start a family

John married Margaret in 1833. The dates in her church obituary make him about twenty-six and her about fourteen. Their marriage lasted more than fifty years.

They raised a large family in Washington Parish. We follow their son Seaborn, who would become Christine’s grandfather.

Their son, Seaborn

Seaborn L. Alford was born in May 1844 and grew up in John and Margaret’s household.

1850 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

John’s farm

John’s farm produced corn and oats. The 1850 farm return associated with him also lists four horses.

Most of its reported acreage was classed as unimproved: six hundred acres, compared with forty acres of improved land.

1862 · Camp Moore · Port Hudson

Seaborn goes to war

In May 1862, John and Margaret’s son Seaborn enlisted in the Confederate army, just before his eighteenth birthday. He joined at Camp Moore and served in a unit that became the 3rd Louisiana Cavalry.

By the following summer, he was at Port Hudson, where Union forces were besieging the Confederate position.

Captured at Port Hudson

Seaborn was captured at Port Hudson on 9 July 1863 and released on parole a few days later.

The photograph shows Union artillery during the siege—the forces opposing him. It gives a view of the war he experienced, although it does not show Seaborn or his unit.

Seaborn marries Laura

After the war, Seaborn married Laura Ellzey on Christmas Eve, 1866. Both were about twenty-two.

They had ten children, according to his later obituary. One was Esco, the son through whom this story reaches Christine. Laura was probably the daughter of John and Elizabeth Ellzey.

1885 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

Margaret’s life in the churches

Margaret had been married to John for more than fifty years when she died in September 1885. Her church remembered a large family: a husband, fourteen surviving children and forty-three grandchildren.

It also recorded her religious life. She joined the Methodists in 1853, was baptized at Mt. Pisgah Baptist Church in 1866, and later joined Bogue Chitto church.

1919 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

A life spent near home

Seaborn died at home in November 1919, aged seventy-five. His obituary placed the house three miles south of Warnerton and said he died “in sight of the place of his birth.”

He had served in a war and raised children from two marriages, yet his home was still close to where he had been born. Christine, his granddaughter, would be born a little over a year after his death.

PART 4: Education, marriage & work

1941 · New Orleans, Louisiana

Christine becomes a nurse

By age twenty, Christine had trained as a nurse in New Orleans. She graduated from Touro Infirmary’s nursing school in August 1941.

Her education took her from the Mount Hermon farming community to a city hospital. This earlier photograph shows Touro around 1910, before her time there.

1941 · New Orleans · Mount Hermon

Her father brings her home

After Christine’s graduation, Esco drove to New Orleans to bring her home. The hometown paper reported a three-week vacation before she returned to Touro to work as a nurse.

Her working life was beginning in the city, while her family remained in the Mount Hermon community.

1942–1943 · Baton Rouge, Louisiana

Public-health work in Baton Rouge

By 1942, Christine was working for the Parish Health Unit in Baton Rouge. The city directory lists her as a nurse there in both 1942 and 1943.

After training in a hospital, she had moved into public-health work. Mount Hermon, New Orleans and Baton Rouge mark the places where she grew up, trained and worked.

Three days after Christine’s marriage date, her mother, Mary Lou, died at home near Mount Hermon. It was 4 February 1946.

Esco’s place in the community

Esco was a farmer and dairyman. He was also a church deacon and served as a parish jury commissioner.

PART 5: Christine’s later life

1975 · Louisiana

Thirty-three years in public health

Christine retired in 1975 after thirty-three years with the Louisiana Public Health Department, according to her later obituary.

Her work included coordinating clinics for children with disabilities across several parishes.

2008 · Christine’s family

Christine’s life, and the generations before her

Christine died in 2008. She had spent thirty-three years in public health, been married to Charles for forty-four years, and served with the Trinity Episcopal Church Altar Guild. Charles died before her.

Her father Esco, grandfather Seaborn and great-grandfather John had farmed in the Bogue Chitto area. Her own working life took her to New Orleans and Baton Rouge.
