THE ALFORDS · 1717–2008

The Alford family

Christine Alford Knight grew up in a farming family near Mount Hermon, Louisiana.

Her father’s family had lived near the Bogue Chitto River for generations. This story follows their likely beginnings in colonial North Carolina, their move south, and the lives they built in Louisiana.

1717–1771 · North Carolina

Julius Alford of North Carolina

Julius Alford was a landowner beside the Tar River in colonial North Carolina. He was probably born in New Kent County, Virginia, in September 1717, the son of James Alford.

He was married to Lucy and had seven children. His son Jacob is probably the man we follow south toward the family’s later home in Louisiana.

1768–1771 · North Carolina

Julius provides for his family

Julius died by 1771. His will provided for Lucy and the children, including money from the estate’s income for their upkeep and schooling.

Jacob was to inherit the home plantation on both sides of the Tar River, along with livestock and a breeding mare. This was the family property he would later sell as he moved away.

The Georgia years

Jacob’s likely route south ran through Montgomery County, Georgia, before reaching the Bogue Chitto region in 1807.

The Jacob Alford living in Georgia between 1797 and 1806 held land and served on juries. In 1798, he was also taxed for two enslaved people, whose names were not recorded.

December 1807 · Bogue Chitto · Spanish West Florida

Settling beside the Bogue Chitto

Jacob settled beside the Bogue Chitto River, just south of today’s Louisiana–Mississippi line. His land claim dates the settlement to December 1807.

This was still Spanish West Florida. The United States had made the Louisiana Purchase four years earlier, but Spain still governed this disputed region.

Here the family put down roots. The later generations we follow would live within a few miles of this tract.

1810–1812 · Bogue Chitto region

The government changes

In 1810, a rebellion against Spanish rule was followed by United States annexation of this part of West Florida. Louisiana became a state in 1812, and the region was incorporated into it that year.

Families already living here now faced a practical question: how would the new government recognize their land claims?

1813–1819 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

From settlement to recognized claim

Jacob’s claim rested on settlement: he had occupied the land before receiving federal recognition of his right to it. A government list compiled in 1813 records his claim.

In 1819, Congress allowed qualifying settlers on these lists to receive land grants—even without written title—if they had lived on or cultivated the land before the required date. The limit was generally one tract of 640 acres.

Jacob’s claim was confirmed under that law. The survey would come later.

1850 · Bogue Chitto, Louisiana

John’s farm

John appears to have been farming 640 acres in 1850, growing corn and oats and keeping four horses. Forty acres were classed as improved and six hundred as unimproved.

Eight years later, his federal land grants covered only about ninety-four acres. The records do not tell us how that earlier farm related to these later holdings; its location remains unknown.

Meet the family

Margaret Brumfield Alford

1819–1885 · Christine’s great-grandmother

Margaret was John Alford’s wife and Christine’s great-grandmother. Her church obituary says they married in 1833, when she was about fourteen. At her death in 1885, it counted a husband, fourteen surviving children and forty-three grandchildren.

She joined the Methodists in 1853, was baptized into Mt. Pisgah Baptist Church in 1866 and later joined Bogue Chitto church.

Brumfield also appears as her maiden name in indexes to five of her children’s death records, with spelling variations. Her church obituary calls her Margaret Alford. Their son Seaborn was Christine’s grandfather.

Mary Lou Fussell Alford

1883 or 1884–1946 · Christine’s mother

Mary Lou Fussell Alford, also called Lou, was Christine’s mother. She married Esco in December 1899, and they made their home in the farming community near Mount Hermon. In 1930, their household included sixteen-year-old Tolbert and nine-year-old Christine.

Her father was Henry T. Fussell. Eliza or Liza Hobbs is her likely mother, although the death indexes disagree on the mother’s name. Her own birth year is also uncertain: records give 1883 and 1884.

She died at home on 4 February 1946, three days after Christine’s marriage date. Her obituary names her daughter as “Mrs. Christine Knight.”

Esco Seaborn Alford

1878–1958 · Christine’s father

Esco was Christine’s father, a farmer and dairyman near Mount Hermon. He was the son of Seaborn and Laura Alford. He also served his community as a church deacon and parish jury commissioner.

He married Mary Lou Fussell in 1899. By 1900 they had their own farm household, near other members of his family. Their daughter Christine later trained as a nurse in New Orleans.

After Mary Lou died in 1946, he married Lynnie Schilling, who died in 1947, and then Florence Bennett in 1951. He died after a stroke in April 1958 and was buried at Bogue Chitto Baptist Church.

Christine Alford Knight

About 1921–2008 · The descendant followed in this story

Christine grew up on a farm near Mount Hermon, the daughter of Esco and Mary Lou Alford. She graduated from Touro Infirmary’s nursing school in 1941 and was working in public health in Baton Rouge by 1942.

She married Charles Binford Knight in 1946 and continued nursing. Her work included coordinating clinics for children with disabilities across several parishes. She retired in 1975 after thirty-three years with the Louisiana Public Health Department, according to her obituary.

She died in 2008, having outlived Charles. Her obituary gives her birth as 1 January 1921. Accounts disagree on her exact death date.

Laura A. Ellzey Alford

1844–1903, reported · Christine’s grandmother

Laura was Seaborn Alford’s first wife, Esco’s mother and Christine’s grandmother. She and Seaborn married on Christmas Eve, 1866, and had ten children, according to his later obituary.

She was probably the daughter of John and Elizabeth Ellzey. Her surname appears in several spellings; Seaborn’s obituary calls her Laura A. Ellzy.

Laura died in 1903, after more than thirty-six years of marriage.

Charles Binford Knight

Married 1946 · Christine’s husband

Charles Binford Knight was Christine’s husband. They married in Bienville Parish on 1 February 1946 and later lived in Baton Rouge.

In 1950, he was an assistant manager at a funeral home while Christine worked as a parish health nurse. Her obituary describes a forty-four-year marriage and says that he died before her.

Seaborn L. Alford

1844–1919 · Christine’s grandfather

Seaborn was a farmer near Warnerton and Christine’s grandfather. He was John and Margaret’s son. He served in the Confederate cavalry, was captured at Port Hudson in 1863 and was released on parole.

He married Laura Ellzey in 1866. They had ten children, according to his obituary; Esco, Christine’s father, was one of them. After Laura’s death, Seaborn married Candacy Bankston and had three more children.

His homestead lay south of his father’s land. When he died in 1919, his obituary said his home was “in sight of the place of his birth.”

John Seaborn Alford

1807–1891 or 1897 · Christine’s great-grandfather

John was a Washington Parish farmer and Christine’s great-grandfather. He married Margaret in 1833, and they raised a large family. Their land lay near the Bogue Chitto River; one of John’s lots later contained the family cemetery.

He had a twin brother, Seaborn John, who became a Methodist preacher. John’s own son was also named Seaborn. This younger Seaborn was Christine’s grandfather.

Their father was probably Julius Alford’s son Jacob, from North Carolina. John’s death year is also unsettled: later sources give 1891 and 1897.

Margaret Alford’s obituary

Selected transcription

Marriage and children

Sister Alford was born in Washington parish. La., February 9th, 1819; aged 66 years, 6 months and 29 days. She married John S. Alford in the year 1833 and raised a large family of children.

End of first page, death

where she remained a faithful member until September 8, 1885, when in the triumphs of faith she fell asleep in the arms of a blessed Jesus.

The image joins two printed pages. The obituary gives no maiden name.

Margaret’s obituary appears in the 1885 minutes of the Magee’s Creek Baptist Association. It reports her birth in Washington Parish on 9 February 1819, marriage to John S. Alford in 1833 and death on 8 September 1885.

It recounts her Methodist membership from 1853, baptism into Mt. Pisgah Baptist Church in 1866 and later move to Bogue Chitto church. The husband, fourteen children and forty-three grandchildren it names as mourners are survivors at her death, rather than a count of every child born.

The displayed extract joins the end of one page to the continuation on the next. The early birth and marriage dates are later recollections in this church account. It calls her Margaret Alford; it does not give her maiden name.

Indexes to five of her children’s death records also give her maiden name as Brumfield or a spelling variant, often with the given name Peggy. One index instead calls the mother “Cynthia Brumfield,” a first-name conflict that the index does not resolve.

Spanish West Florida becomes part of Louisiana

In 1807, the Bogue Chitto tract lay in Spanish West Florida. The United States claimed the region under its interpretation of the 1803 Louisiana Purchase, while Spain continued to govern it.

Settlers rebelled against Spain in 1810. The United States then annexed the area between the Mississippi and Pearl rivers. Louisiana became a state on 30 April 1812; the Florida Parishes were incorporated into the state later in 1812.

Jacob’s family could remain beside the Bogue Chitto as the territory around them changed governments.

Jacob, Elizabeth and Franky

The 1968 newspaper transcription of a copied family Bible identifies Elizabeth Bryant as Jacob Alford’s wife and the mother of Nedom Judge Alford, usually called Needham. It gives his birth as 12 July 1789 and Jacob’s death as 16 July 1824.

Later family histories place Jacob’s first marriage to Elizabeth around 1785 in North Carolina and a second marriage to Frances Seaborn around 1792 in Cumberland County. The marriage dates and Frances’s maiden name are uncertain. What happened to Elizabeth remains unresolved.

Franky B. Allford is named in the 1830 Washington Parish census. Her identification as Jacob’s widow is supported by the household sequence. Her birthplace and parents are uncertain.

The Bible transcription’s Jacob is probably the man followed through the migration story, but it supplies no North Carolina birthplace or direct link to Julius. Its 1824 death would make the 1807 twins almost seventeen, in tension with the 1884 obituary’s statement that they were quite small when their father died.

How Jacob’s settlement became a land claim

Cosby’s list records Jacob Alford with one claim “by settlement,” beginning in December 1807. The list was certified in June 1813. Its settlement date need not be the day Jacob first arrived in the region.

Section 3 of the 1819 act entitled qualifying claimants or their legal representatives on the commissioners’ lists to a donation grant if the land had been inhabited or cultivated on or before 15 April 1813, even without written evidence of claim. It limited the grant to one tract of no more than 640 acres and excluded land recognized under the preceding sections.

Jacob’s later survey records cite confirmation under the act, certificate 41 and claim 987 on Cosby’s list. The 1831 survey for his heirs gives 636.54 acres. A later resurvey gives 641.32 acres; these are successive survey measurements, not separate claims.

Jacob settled first; federal recognition followed. Whether he had earlier Spanish permission is unknown.
