1768 · North Carolina

Julius Alford’s will

Julius Alford left his son Jacob the home plantation on both sides of the Tar River in North Carolina. His will was written in 1768 and proved in 1771.

This is a possible beginning for Christine’s Louisiana line. The connection between this Jacob and the later Louisiana family remains unproved.

Meet the family

Margaret Brumfield Alford

1819–1885 · Christine’s great-grandmother

Margaret married John S. Alford in 1833, according to her church obituary. Family and census records support them as Seaborn L.’s parents.

Her obituary records successive memberships in the Methodist church, Mt. Pisgah Baptist Church and Bogue Chitto church. At her death on 8 September 1885, it counted fourteen surviving children and forty-three grandchildren.

Mary Lou Fussell Alford

1883 or 1884–1946 · Christine’s mother

Mary Lou, often called Lou in the records, married Esco Alford on 28 December 1899, according to a compiled marriage index. Her own obituary names her daughter Christine Knight.

The records support Henry T. Fussell as her father. Eliza or Liza Hobbs is the likely mother, based on indexes that are not entirely consistent. Mary Lou died on 4 February 1946. Her birth year remains unresolved.

Esco Seaborn Alford

1878–1958 · Christine’s father

Esco was a farmer and dairyman, the son of Seaborn L. and Laura Alford. His own draft card gives his birth as 21 May 1878.

He married Mary Lou Fussell in 1899. Their daughter Christine trained as a nurse. After Mary Lou died in 1946, Esco married Lynnie Schilling, who died in 1947, and later Florence Bennett. The later marriage dates come from compiled indexes. His death on 15 April 1958 is inferred from the newspaper’s issue date and weekday.

Christine Alford Knight

About 1921–2008 · The descendant followed in this story

Christine was the daughter of Esco and Mary Lou Alford. She graduated from Touro Infirmary’s school of nursing in 1941 and worked in public health in Baton Rouge.

Her marriage to Charles Binford Knight is indexed on 1 February 1946. Her later obituary reports retirement in 1975 after thirty-three years with the Louisiana Public Health Department. It gives her birth as 1 January 1921; an original birth record has not been reached. Only her death year, 2008, is used because the held accounts disagree on the exact date.

Laura A. Ellzey Alford

1844–1903, reported · Christine’s grandmother

Laura married Seaborn L. Alford on 24 December 1866, according to his later obituary. It reports ten children from their marriage; household and family evidence supports her as Esco’s mother.

She was probably the daughter of John and Elizabeth Ellzey. Her death on 19 March 1903 is reported by a cemetery transcription; the held photograph does not independently resolve the worn digits.

Charles Binford Knight

Married 1946 · Christine’s husband

Charles B. Knight married Christine Alford on 1 February 1946, according to the Louisiana marriage index. The 1950 census places them in Baton Rouge and records his work as an assistant manager at a funeral home.

Christine’s obituary describes a forty-four-year marriage and says that he predeceased her.

Seaborn L. Alford

1844–1919 · Christine’s grandfather

Seaborn was the son of John and Margaret, according to the combined census and family evidence. He served in the Confederate cavalry and was captured and paroled at Port Hudson in 1863.

He married Laura A. Ellzy, usually spelled Ellzey, in 1866. Esco was one of their children. After Laura’s death, he married Candacy Bankston in 1909; his obituary reports three children from that marriage.

John Seaborn Alford

1807–1891 or 1897 · Christine’s great-grandfather

John was Margaret Brumfield’s husband and a Washington Parish farmer. Census households and later family records support them as the parents of Seaborn L. Alford.

His twin brother’s obituary gives their birth as 11 October 1807 and names their father Jacob. It does not settle which Jacob. Later sources disagree about John’s death year.

How this landscape was made

The map combines the held modern river trace with historical tracts reconstructed in the project atlas. Positions derive from the 1851 township plat fitted to the modern township outline. Small lot boundaries are approximate, generally about ±50 metres. No houses or travelled routes have been invented.

The atlas measures about 3.4 miles in a straight line from the centre of Jacob’s claim to Seaborn’s homestead. The main text rounds this to about three and a half miles. The land spans several sections of the same township.

John’s and Seaborn’s colours distinguish generations. The broken boundary around Jacob’s claim accompanies a possible earlier family connection. Outlines accumulate as dated land records; they do not establish uninterrupted possession or inheritance of one tract.

Images shown in the passage are held historical records; texture, highlighting and typography provide the presentation.
